\noindent\textbf{Title:} BYANAGA: Performance Evaluation of a Smart Tourism Management Platform for Improved Tourist Experience and Services

\noindent\textbf{Researchers:} Una, Justin, E., Borela, Rene Richard S., Mira, Ace Ben C., and Moreno, Jerome Ford G.

\noindent\textbf{Institution:} College of Computer Studies, Naga College Foundation, Inc.

This study presents BYANAGA, a web-based Smart Tourism Destination Management Platform developed to address the operational gaps in Naga City's tourism ecosystem. The study responds to three documented problems: the absence of a centralized platform for tourists to discover available vouchers, discounts, and promotions from local establishments; the lack of a structured digital promotion channel for establishment owners; and the fragmented coordination between the Naga City Tourism Office and the private tourism sector. Grounded in the Smart Tourism Information Systems Framework, Digital Transformation and Platform Ecosystems Theory, and the Extended Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology 2 (UTAUT2), the platform integrates four role-differentiated interfaces — Tourist, Establishment Owner, Tourism Officer, and Administrator — within a centralized relational database. Core components include a tourist-facing establishment and voucher discovery interface, an establishment owner management portal with voucher submission and revision workflows, a tourism officer governance panel for registration approval and report generation, and an administrator dashboard for system-wide oversight. The platform is evaluated using the System Usability Scale (SUS) and User Acceptance Testing (UAT) involving thirty (30) tourists, five (5) establishment owners, and one (1) tourism officer as respondents.

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} smart tourism platform, voucher management system, tourist-establishment connectivity, local government digitalization, Naga City, web-based system, usability evaluation

\chapter{Introduction}

\section{Background of the Study}

Tourism is one of the important contributors to economic growth and community development in many countries, including the Philippines. With the rapid advancement of technology, tourism industries are now shifting from traditional systems into digital and smart tourism platforms that provide faster, more organized, and more convenient services for tourists and tourism stakeholders. Smart tourism uses information and communication technologies (ICT), cloud systems, mobile applications, artificial intelligence, and real-time data services to improve tourist experiences, tourism management, and destination sustainability \cite{galvao2024}. According to Yin et al.\ \cite{yin2022}, Tourism Cloud Management Systems (TCMS) enhance tourist experiences and provide access to shared destination data, enabling tourists to discover local attractions, read peer reviews, and participate in local festivities through smart tourism models. These digital innovations allow tourists to access information easily, book services online, navigate destinations efficiently, and receive real-time updates during their travels \cite{qi2021}.

Globally, tourism organizations and researchers emphasize the importance of digital transformation in tourism management. Smart tourism systems are considered effective tools for improving visitor satisfaction, increasing operational efficiency, and supporting sustainable tourism development \cite{tavitiyaman2021}. Studies also show that responsive and user-friendly tourism platforms can positively influence tourists' behavioral intentions, engagement, and revisit decisions \cite{park2021}. Furthermore, technologies such as AI-powered recommendation systems, cloud-based tourism platforms, and predictive analytics can help tourism organizations deliver personalized services and improve destination competitiveness \cite{vavpotic2021}. Sampaio et al.\ \cite{sampaio2025} further noted that digital twin and smart city technologies are increasingly being applied in tourism management to improve destination planning and visitor experience, although usability and system evaluation remain key research gaps.

In the Philippines, the government continues to promote digitalization through programs and policies related to smart cities and digital tourism. The Department of Information and Communications Technology (DICT) introduced the Digital Cities 2025 initiative to strengthen digital infrastructure and innovation across the country \cite{dict2020}. The Department of Tourism (DOT) also supports sustainable and technology-driven tourism development to improve tourism services and destination management \cite{dot2022}. Several local government units are now integrating smart tourism concepts into their tourism programs to improve tourist engagement and economic opportunities \cite{dost2024}.

Naga City, recognized as one of the emerging smart cities in the Philippines, continues to strengthen its digital transformation initiatives through technology-based programs and smart governance projects \cite{naga2025}. However, despite the growing adoption of digital tourism systems in other regions, there is still a need to evaluate how tourism platforms can improve tourist experiences, accessibility, responsiveness, and sustainability within local destinations. Challenges such as system usability, service reliability, information accessibility, and platform responsiveness remain important concerns that need to be addressed \cite{ramos2024}.

This study focuses on the development and evaluation of a smart tourism platform designed to improve tourism services and visitor experiences. Thestudy aims to examine how digital tourism technologies, platform responsiveness, usability, and smart service integration can contribute to tourism management and sustainable tourism development. By integrating modern tourism technologies and user-centered design approaches, the proposed system seeks to provide a more efficient, accessible, and engaging tourism experience for tourists and tourism stakeholders \cite{gretzel2023}.

\section{Review of Related Literature}

\subsection{Digital Voucher and Promotional Management Systems in Tourism}

The use of digital voucher management and promotional mechanisms has proven vital for creating connections between establishments and tourists by providing efficient promotional mechanics in today's tourism systems. Albu proves that real-time visibility of available offers through centralized digital platforms reduces transactional friction between the service provider and consumer by streamlining the discovery of promotions \cite{albu2025}. Tourists who access destination promotions via digital platforms display considerably higher engagement levels compared to tourists using other non-centralized digital or traditional methods. Kim et al. show that smart tourism mechanisms such as the use of ICT, mobile and cloud-based applications increase tourist satisfaction due to the provision of real-time and organized promotion mechanisms, where tourists using centralized platforms displaying promotions from various establishments engage more than those accessing promotions from a single establishment \cite{kim2021}.

The issue of voucher governance design is another constant among the recent works. According to Ahn, Kim, and Lee, multi-stakeholder platforms where the process involves an approval stage, meaning that the promotional vouchers are checked by a tourism office before their publication on tourist-oriented websites, lead to better content quality and increased tourist confidence compared to freely accessible directory services \cite{ahn2022}. In his work, Ahn, Kim, and Lee distinguish officer-based approval as the main difference between successful destination platforms with well-performed promotions and inconsistent promotion platforms \cite{ahn2022}. Albu proves that real-time visibility of the voucher's status, which allows for checking whether the submitted voucher is still pending, undergoing review, or approved, reduces administrative efforts and speeds up the publication process \cite{albu2025}. Moreover, El Archi et al. provide systematic literature evidence from smart tourism destinations about the efficiency of officer-based content management processes \cite{elarchi2023}.

Promotion redemption is an integral part of ensuring the effectiveness of vouchers and accurate data collection. According to Zhang, Li, and Hua, the use of cloud-based tourism promotion platforms helps monitor the redemption process, offering immediate feedback on the results of tourism promotions for both merchants and authorities involved in managing the area \cite{zhang2022}. Moreover, Suanpang et al. argue that AI integrated platforms can analyze the redemption process to derive insights about the behavior of visitors at a specific location \cite{suanpang2024}. Concerning the issue in the Philippine setting, Cruz argues that there is a problem in the ranking of smart tourism in the Philippines compared to its neighboring Southeast Asian countries because of insufficient digital infrastructure related to promotions \cite{cruz2024}. Furthermore, Nuñez and Borbon argue that the owners of establishments in Camarines Sur region rely only on informal channels for promotions since no formalized digital system exists in the local tourism office \cite{nunez2022}. Finally, Buena and Borbon confirm that destination image and tourist engagement are dependent on promotional information provided through informal channels \cite{buena2022}.

Notification mechanisms built into the voucher systems have implications for the level of workflow efficiency achieved. The study conducted by DOST and the City Government of Naga reveals the reliance of smart city information systems on notification system infrastructure for maintaining consistency in operations among several different agencies—a concept easily adaptable to tourism platform construction \cite{dost2024,naga2024}. Garces illustrates how Smart Tourism Technologies in Philippine destinations generate tangible gains in tourist experiences from the implementation of promotion discovery processes coupled with communication mechanisms that notify tourism authorities and business owners \cite{garces2025}. Predictive capabilities through the incorporation of redemption and analytics in platforms provide an advantage to tourism managers in forecasting the risk of satisfaction issues for tourists while optimizing promotional activities, as explained by Hidayat and Amin \cite{hidayat2023}. Buhalis and Amaranggana prove that connected destination platforms delivering real-time redemption and foot traffic analytics generate high-quality promotion \cite{amaranggana2023}.

The revision cycle, which involves the tourism officer giving structured feedback instead of rejecting the voucher directly, is highlighted by Cabrera and Mendoza as an ideal approach to ensuring continued collaboration between the tourism officers and businesses within the private sector in the Philippines, as the resubmission rate would be much higher with such an approach \cite{cabrera2024}. According to Park et al., the contactless system and verification system based on codes, which have already been established in Philippine tourism using QR technology for payments, ensure the necessary user familiarity with the system for voucher code redemption systems \cite{park2022}. Nicart, Arellano, and Acunin highlight the fact that systems requiring the registration and submission of documents by the enterprise in advance prior to promotion ensure higher credibility of the offered promotions \cite{nicart2024}. Evangelista reveals that systems of digital marketing that involve formal registration processes yield greater tourist involvement compared to informal social networks-based promotions \cite{evangelista2022}. Rocamora points out that there are existing examples of verified digital promotions for the country from the experience of other countries \cite{rocamora2022}.

Security, privacy, and compliance issues are critical considerations when designing tourism voucher platforms for Philippine local government units. The Digital Payments Roadmap by the Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas outlines standards for secure transactions that define authentication requirements for tourism voucher platforms \cite{bsp2024}. The Philippine Statistics Authority notes that tourism information systems must conform to the provisions of the Data Privacy Act of 2012 \cite{psa2025}. Kerpedzhiev et al. assert that digital platforms used as governance infrastructure should incorporate security protocols from the inception of their design \cite{kerpedzhiev2021}. Carlisle, Ivanov, and Dijkmans show that the success of platforms is not only dependent on technical functionality but on the proper definition of governance and data-sharing responsibilities among stakeholders \cite{carlisle2021}. It is further noted that digital coordination formalization involving the cooperation of LGUs and tourism companies is explicitly mentioned in the NEDA Bicol Regional Development Plan \cite{neda2023}. As per Wang, Li, and Zhang, the adoption and usage rates are high for platforms whose designs are in line with strategic sustainability goals \cite{wang2024}. Furthermore, MDPI supports that platforms designed to serve the SDG-oriented governance purposes enjoy the backing of stakeholders \cite{mdpi2025}. Finally, the World Tourism Organization stipulates that destination management platforms that incorporate promotional management and visitor management are the current benchmarks for digital DMO governance practices \cite{unwto2023}.

\subsection{Web-Based Tourism Platforms for Tourist-Establishment Connectivity}

Digital platforms have emerged to be the main technology used in connecting travelers with local enterprises within destinations around the globe. According to Kim, Lee, and Ham, personalization and interface feedback of a destination website influence revisit intention and destination loyalty positively, and an establishment profile has a direct impact on tourist engagement \cite{kim2023}. Buhalis argues that technology innovations bring the entire range of tourism stakeholders together in service ecosystems, and that smart platforms are now the central infrastructure for connecting travelers with local service providers \cite{buhalis2020}. UN Tourism confirms that digital tools are now recognized globally as indispensable instruments for revitalizing tourism economies and improving connectivity between destinations and their visitors \cite{untourism2024}.

Establishment profile management capacities lie at the core of successful connectivity. According to Li and Fang, AI-driven recommendation engines rely heavily on the accuracy and consistency of information presented in establishment profiles created by business owners, thus incentivizing business owners to maintain an extensive digital footprint \cite{li2024}. As per Santos and Correia, multi-channel platforms integrating description information, picture gallery, and events information provide higher user engagement than text-based ones, hence confirming the choice of BYANAGA to use visual cards as part of establishment profiles \cite{santos2023}. Lopez and Ortega argue further that experience-based travel destinations mitigate expectation-experience discrepancy with the help of accurate presentation of establishments' content and available services and discounts at present \cite{lopez2024}. Xiang, Stienmetz, and Fesenmaier demonstrate that smart tourism design, which integrates accurate and rich resource information from establishments, forms the conceptual foundation for the development of competitive smart destinations \cite{xiang2021}.

As far as the Philippines is concerned, the Philippine Development Plan highlights investment in digital tourism platforms linking LGUs' tourism departments to tourism businesses as a key regional tourism competitiveness priority \cite{neda2022}. As underlined by the DOT Philippine Tourism Development Plan, digital systems facilitating equal promotion of tourism businesses of all sizes are explicitly needed, which directly confirms BYANAGA's strategy to give equal access to all registered establishments \cite{dot2023}. Del Vecchio et al. demonstrate that smart tourism platforms which embed governance structures into their establishment listing and content management processes deliver measurably higher sustainable performance outcomes than those without formal content governance \cite{delvecchio2022}. Event listings integrated into establishment pages further improve customer engagement with platforms: as Zhang, Li, and Hua show, cloud-IoT platforms that combine establishment information with real-time event and promotional content generate significantly higher tourist interaction rates than static directories \cite{zhang2022cloud}.

Mobile-responsiveness in website design will serve best when implementing tourist and establishment connectivity in secondary Philippine cities. Mobile access to the Internet is identified as the main means of connectivity used by tourists and establishments in cities such as Naga as per the DICT Free Wi-Fi for All Program \cite{dictwifi}. Mobile responsiveness for tourist engagement is also supported by the findings of PricewaterhouseCoopers showing greater customer engagement in tourism businesses using mobile-responsive websites \cite{pwc2021}. Tourist demand in Bicol region destinations for centralization of digital information regarding events is also reported by the Philippine Information Agency, which proves the validity of integrating an event calendar into BYANAGA \cite{pia2023}. Tourism offices managing digital tourism platforms are found in DOT Region V to create accurate digital identity representations of a destination; higher customer satisfaction levels are achieved when regional tourism offices actively manage their destination's digital directory \cite{dotregionv2024}. TPB supports this finding, noting that responsiveness and quality of platform management directly correlate with visitor satisfaction outcomes at the national level \cite{tpb2023}.

Data sharing between tourism offices and establishment owners by means of platform participation frameworks helps fill the coordination gap reported from tourism operations in the Bicol region. Say found that smart tourism readiness — encompassing digital infrastructure and service innovation — significantly and positively affects visitor satisfaction in Philippine urban destinations, underscoring the need for institutionally governed data-sharing mechanisms between public tourism bodies and local enterprises \cite{say2025}. According to the Asian Development Bank, platform implementations in secondary cities of Southeast Asia where platform governance was institutionalized achieved higher sustainability and involved less administrative work \cite{adb2025}. Hidayat and Amin find that formalized data-sharing arrangements within tourism engagement platforms in Asia-Pacific destinations lead to better alignment between promotional activities and visitor demand patterns \cite{hidayat2023a}. Buhalis and Amaranggana further show that smart destination systems with embedded inter-stakeholder data governance produce consistently higher platform performance outcomes than those where data responsibilities remain informal \cite{buhalis2023}.

The ultimate proof lies in tourism satisfaction levels and economic outcomes from digital connectivity platforms. The World Travel and Tourism Council Philippines Report indicates that digital tourism infrastructure investments deliver quantifiable economic gains via higher tourist expenditure and longer stay periods \cite{wttc2025}. European Smart Tourism Destinations frameworks reveal that destination connectivity platforms at the city level that achieve the governance structure embedded in BYANAGA constitute the present-day international standard for urban tourism digital transformation \cite{european2022}. Hidayat's research shows that smart platform analytics linking visitor flow predictions to operational dashboards enable destination managers to make proactive resource allocation decisions that improve service quality \cite{hidayat2023b}. Jarupunphol, Arpornpisal, and Rittippant confirm that tourism platform adoption is significantly driven by performance expectancy and hedonic motivation among users, reinforcing the importance of BYANAGA's design in facilitating genuine tourist and establishment engagement \cite{jarupunphol2025}. Wang, Li, and Zhang also confirm that digital platforms whose design is compatible with the strategic sustainability objectives of their hosts will have higher adoption rates and continued use compared to those not aligned with organizational objectives, affirming the design of BYANAGA with respect to equitable tourism development \cite{wang2024b}.

\subsection{Usability Evaluation and Performance Assessment of Tourism Web Platforms}

Web-based tourism platform evaluation has become an advanced multi-method field that includes usability tools, performance testing, and contextual evaluation of user acceptance. For tourism platforms that cater to different user groups — such as BYANAGA, which caters to tourists, business operators, and the tourism officer — role differentiation in administering the System Usability Scale is advised by Gretzel, considering that usability of the tourist interface differs from that of the governance panel, and each should be evaluated using a different process \cite{gretzel2023}. Recent studies further emphasize that usability remains a primary determinant of tourism website success, particularly in systems involving promotional discovery, navigability, and user-centered service integration \cite{tsegaye2021,atiya2021}.
 
The User Acceptance Test plays a complementary role to established measures for assessing usability. Alalwan, Dwivedi, and Rana point out that while conducting tests among regular users performing role-specific tasks, usability problems emerge that cannot be captured using standardized surveys alone \cite{utauttourism2023}. In the context of tourism websites in the Philippines, Satzinger, Jackson, and Burd note that UAT must account for users' varying levels of digital readiness, as acceptability depends on successful task performance even among users with limited technical background \cite{satzinger2021}. Additional usability studies on tourism websites found that poor navigation, inconsistent object naming, and overwhelming interface layouts significantly reduce task completion efficiency and overall tourist satisfaction \cite{huang2020,martinez2020}. In evaluating the performance metrics of websites,both technical and experiential aspects need to be considered at once. According to Pressman and Maxim, platform acceptability is governed by response time standards: under one second for optimal performance, one to three seconds for acceptable performance, and beyond three seconds performance degrades to the point that users abandon tasks at rates of 20 to 40 percent \cite{pressman2020}. Moreover, according to Del Vecchio et al., the evaluation of the performance of tourism websites involves assessing data accuracy apart from response times, because inaccuracy can be more damaging than slowness to tourists' confidence \cite{delvecchio2022}.

Criteria for data accuracy for platforms that will manage the tourism industry in LGUs are provided by Mariani and Borghi, who explain that redemption data management platforms must be able to generate accuracy rates of greater than 95 percent before any implementation can take place \cite{mariani2022}. This is essential to ensure that analysis dashboards provide accurate results for the benefit of tourism officers as well as establishment proprietors. Xiang argues that digital experience benchmarking in smart tourism destinations requires the inclusion of data accuracy metrics in addition to usability ratings, because the platform's value in governance decision-making is solely dependent on the accuracy of the aggregated data \cite{xiang2022}. Similarly, Li finds that data accuracy ensures significantly better decision-making from users in integrated tourism services using AI-based performance scoring, provided that data accuracy remains at 95 percent or above \cite{li2023}. ISO/IEC 25010 further establishes that functional correctness — encompassing accuracy, completeness, and appropriateness of outputs — is a fundamental quality characteristic that platform evaluations must assess alongside usability \cite{iso25010}. Contemporary ISO/IEC 25010-based evaluations also highlight the importance of reliability, portability, and performance efficiency in modern tourism information systems \cite{maja2024,isoeval2020}. Gretzel and colleagues argue that smart tourism information systems require evaluation of both their data layer and their user interface layer simultaneously, as weaknesses in either layer independently degrade the platform's overall service value \cite{smarttourism2022}. Platform evaluation in a Philippine environment necessitates considering specific circumstances surrounding use. According to the Camarines Sur Provincial Tourism Office, tourism platform performance in the Bicol region is significantly affected by connectivity variations during peak tourist periods, making user load testing an important performance measure in evaluating BYANAGA \cite{camarinessur2025}. Sommerville argues that expert validation conducted as a preliminary step before user testing allows domain experts to analyze platform architecture and design workflows before exposure to end users, thereby minimizing workflow errors that UAT participants would likely overlook \cite{sommerville2021}. According to Koo, Chung, and Nam, sustainability performance measures — including resiliency, reliability, and account management — are additional metrics that experts should evaluate before proceeding to UAT \cite{koo2021}. Recent studies on tourism website standardization additionally emphasize the importance of integrating usability engineering principles throughout the entire development lifecycle to improve accessibility and user retention \cite{andag2021,reviewtourism2020}.

The iterative refinement cycle between evaluation phases plays an important role. Chung and Koo support the idea that smart tourism platforms should undergo several iterations of usability testing, because users' mental models for promotion and voucher management interfaces change and mature with experience, rendering a single evaluation round insufficient \cite{chung2022}. Mahmud states that cloud-based tourism platforms can undergo several iterations of testing for uptime and reliability since server-side problems revealed at initial stages do not yet affect the end user \cite{mahmud2022}. Gretzel emphasizes that mixed methodologies combining both quantitative measurements and qualitative evaluations provide the most beneficial suggestions for platform improvement, since quantitative scores reveal the dimensions to improve while qualitative evaluations explain the reasoning behind them \cite{gretzel2023}. According to Martin, incorporating user feedback obtained during evaluation stages into digital feedback loop engineering in tourism platforms results in more usable platforms than what developers' intuition can produce \cite{martin2023}. Du further demonstrates that intelligent tourism information systems that undergo iterative evaluation and refinement based on collected user data achieve significantly higher accuracy and satisfaction scores than those evaluated only once \cite{du2021}.

Evaluation benchmarks for Philippine LGU tourism platforms are derived from the work of Ramos, who found that SUS scores within the range of 72 to 84 represent what should be achieved by well-polished multi-purpose tourism platforms after iterative development — thus providing the benchmark for comparing BYANAGA's evaluation scores \cite{ramos2024}. Mendoza finds that evaluation of Philippine tourism platforms should consider service continuity alongside usability scores, because a platform may score highly in usability but low in reliability, thus being unable to deliver continued value to its tourism offices \cite{mendoza2024}. Estrada finds that tourism platform evaluation conducted under realistic conditions — such as simulated peak tourist load — provides better benchmark performance than evaluation under ideal conditions, a critical consideration for BYANAGA's performance testing process \cite{estrada2025}. Jeong and Shin note that trust factors such as accuracy of data perception, authentication, and visibility of voucher verification processes are among the most effective indicators of adoption of tourism sites \cite{jeong2023}. Thus, the system employed by BYANAGA of having officers approve vouchers can be considered an indicator of trust within the assessment framework. According to Balinas and Oro, online scoring of digital services provided by tourism websites in the Philippines yields better evaluation data when conducted by users who represent the actual target audience \cite{balinas2024}.

\section{Synthesis of the State-of-the-Art}

The reviewed literature across digital voucher and promotional management
systems, web-based tourist-establishment connectivity platforms, and usability
evaluation and performance assessment frameworks reveals a consistent and
convergent direction: effective destination management in the contemporary
tourism landscape requires integrated, governed, and user-centered digital
platforms that simultaneously serve the discovery needs of tourists, the
promotional management needs of local businesses, and the governance and
reporting needs of tourism authorities.

Across the first body of literature, a clear consensus emerges that the
governance architecture of a digital voucher system is as important as its
technical functionality. Studies consistently demonstrate that platforms
embedding an officer-mediated approval layer between establishment-submitted
promotional content and tourist-facing publication produce significantly
higher content quality, greater tourist trust, and more reliable redemption
data than open-access promotional directories \cite{ahn2022, albu2025,
elarchi2023}. The revision workflow, where the tourism officer sends
structured feedback instead of rejecting a voucher outright, is
specifically identified as a best practice for sustaining the collaborative
relationship between governance authorities and private sector businesses
in Philippine LGU contexts \cite{cabrera2024, nunez2022}. Redemption
mechanics, particularly code-based confirmation panels, are shown to
produce more accurate promotional performance data and reduce disputed
claims compared to informal verification methods \cite{zhang2022,
park2022}. The broader Philippine context reveals that the absence of
formalized digital promotion infrastructure at the local tourism office
level is a documented and measurable barrier to equitable promotional
access for small and medium tourism enterprises \cite{cruz2024, nunez2022,
buena2022, evangelista2022}. Security and data governance compliance,
particularly alignment with the Data Privacy Act of 2012 and the
standards established by the Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas, are
identified as non-negotiable foundational requirements for any tourism
platform operating within Philippine LGU governance contexts
\cite{bsp2024, psa2025, kerpedzhiev2021, carlisle2021}.

The second body of literature establishes that the core value of
tourist-establishment connectivity platforms lies in their ability to
reduce information asymmetry between tourists seeking services and
businesses offering them \cite{kim2023, buhalis2020, untourism2024}.
Self-managed establishment profiles with rich visual content, including
descriptions, image galleries, pricing information, and available
promotional offers displayed in card-based layouts, are consistently
identified as the interface design approach that generates the highest
tourist browsing engagement and establishment participation rates
\cite{li2024, santos2023, xiang2021}. The integration of event listings
within establishment profiles is shown to produce higher tourist planning
engagement than either static directories or event calendars operating
independently \cite{zhang2022cloud}. Mobile-responsive web design is
confirmed as the appropriate architecture for Philippine secondary city
deployment contexts, where tourists and small business operators
predominantly access digital services through mobile browsers rather
than desktop workstations \cite{dictwifi, pwc2021}. The formalization
of data-sharing arrangements between tourism offices and the private
sector through platform participation frameworks, instead of informal
coordination channels, is associated with higher platform sustainability,
lower administrative overhead, and better alignment between promotional
activity and actual tourist demand \cite{say2025, adb2025, hidayat2023a,
buhalis2023}. The alignment of platform design with the strategic
sustainability goals of the host institution, specifically the Naga
City Tourism Office's mandate for equitable tourism development,
is identified as a significant predictor of long-term adoption and
sustained usage \cite{wang2024b, jarupunphol2025}.

The third body of literature confirms that comprehensive tourism platform
evaluation requires a multi-instrument, multi-role, and iterative
methodology that no single assessment tool can adequately capture alone
\cite{gretzel2023, delvecchio2022}. The System Usability Scale remains
the most widely validated instrument for measuring perceived usability
across diverse tourism platform contexts, with scores between 72 and 84
representing the established benchmark for well-refined multi-role
platforms following iterative development in Philippine LGU contexts
\cite{ramos2024, mendoza2024}. Role-differentiated administration of
the SUS is recommended for platforms serving multiple user groups,
such as tourists, establishment owners, and tourism officers, because the
usability requirements of consumer-facing interfaces differ substantially
from those of governance and reporting panels \cite{gretzel2023,
satzinger2021}. User Acceptance Testing conducted with representative
users performing role-specific tasks surfaces interaction problems that
standardized instruments cannot detect, making it an indispensable
complement to SUS-based evaluation \cite{utauttourism2023}. Technical
performance assessment must incorporate data accuracy verification
alongside response time benchmarking, since inaccuracies in tourism data
systems undermine the decision-support value of analytics dashboards
more severely than moderate latency increases \cite{delvecchio2022,
mariani2022, xiang2022, iso25010}. Expert validation conducted before
user-based testing reduces the risk of fundamental workflow errors that
UAT participants would overlook within the scope of predefined tasks
\cite{sommerville2021, koo2021}. Iterative refinement between evaluation
rounds, where user feedback from each cycle systematically informs
the next development iteration, is identified as the most reliable
approach to achieving platforms whose final usability and reliability
scores exceed those achievable through single-round evaluation
\cite{chung2022, mahmud2022, martin2023, du2021}. Trust factors,
specifically the transparency and reliability of voucher verification
mechanisms, are among the strongest predictors of tourism platform
adoption in the Philippine context \cite{jeong2023, balinas2024}.

Taken together, the three bodies of literature provide comprehensive
and convergent empirical grounding for the design, governance structure,
and evaluation methodology of BYANAGA. The specific operational gaps
motivating this study, namely the absence of a centralized voucher and
promotion discovery platform for tourists in Naga City, the lack of a
structured digital promotion channel for local establishments, and the
fragmented coordination between the Naga City Tourism Office and the
private tourism sector, are each well-documented in the reviewed
literature as both practically significant and effectively addressable
through a purposefully designed, officer-governed, web-based platform
architecture \cite{cruz2024, nunez2022, buena2022, carlisle2021,
neda2023}. The literature further confirms that meaningful digital
transformation in local tourism governance does not require costly
smart city infrastructure, but rather a platform whose design is
precisely calibrated to the specific operational realities of its
users and the governance context of its deployment \cite{say2025,
adb2025, ramos2024, wang2024b}.

\section{Gap Bridged by the Study}

This research mainly addresses the gaps in the fragmented digital management
processes of tourism in Naga City. The literature review reveals a big
gap in the system, which consists of tourism information, promotional
offers, events, and other services related to establishments being spread
out through different platforms. The fragmented system makes it difficult
for tourists to access promotions and tourism information, as well as less
efficient for stakeholders in the industry. As such, the study recommends
the implementation of the BYANAGA system, a web-based platform for
managing tourism in a more integrated way.

In addition, the study also considers the coordination challenge
highlighted in the previous studies about the absence of an organized
collaboration system between the tourist department and other
establishments in the area. Current procedures depend largely on
informal communication and separate promotional efforts, which lead to
inconsistency in the distribution of information as well as ineffective
data exchange among individuals involved. With the development of the
proposed platform, all parties concerned including tourists, owners of
establishments, tourism department personnel, and city administrators can
interact via the platform through functions including vouchers,
establishment profiles, event announcement, and report generation.

\section{Theoretical Framework}

This study is anchored on contemporary theories that explain the role of integrated digital platforms in smart destination management, stakeholder collaboration, and technology-driven tourism governance.

The primary theory underpinning this study is the Smart Tourism Information Systems Framework \cite{du2021}. This theory explains that intelligent destination management is achieved through the integration of big data analytics, artificial intelligence, IoT devices, and cloud computing infrastructure that transform fragmented tourism information into actionable intelligence. Du \cite{du2021} demonstrates that data mining algorithms, real-time visualization technology, and predictive analytics significantly improve tourism information processing, visitor flow management, and operational decision-making.

The second supporting theory is the Digital Transformation and Platform Ecosystems Theory \cite{kerpedzhiev2021, carlisle2021}. This theory emphasizes that digital platforms function as sociotechnical infrastructures that fundamentally reshape tourism service delivery by enabling multi-stakeholder collaboration, data exchange networks, and value co-creation among government units, local businesses, and tourists. Carlisle et al. \cite{carlisle2021} establish that digital tourism platforms serve as connective infrastructure enabling data exchange and service integration rather than isolated information systems.

The third supporting theory is the Extended Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology 2 (UTAUT2) adapted for tourism contexts \cite{venkatesh2012, jarupunphol2025}. This theory highlights that technology adoption in tourism settings is influenced by performance expectancy, effort expectancy, social influence, facilitating conditions, hedonic motivation, price value, and habit. Jarupunphol et al. \cite{jarupunphol2025} demonstrate that UTAUT2 effectively explains technology adoption in tourism contexts including AI chatbots, mobile applications, and digital itinerary systems.

Together, these three theories enhance the study's focus by aligning technological capabilities, organizational integration, and user acceptance into a unified framework. They collectively explain why a Smart Tourism Destination Management Platform is necessary to bridge digital-physical disconnects, how it should function as both technical infrastructure and collaborative ecosystem, and what conditions are required for its effective adoption in local destination management contexts.

This conceptual framework is anchored on the Input-Process-Output-Outcome (IPOO) model and illustrates the overall flow of the study in designing, developing, and evaluating the BYANAGA Smart Tourism Destination Management Platform for Naga City. The \textbf{Input} phase represents the foundational elements and existing conditions considered in the study. This includes the identification of process and system problems currently experienced in tourism management, such as fragmented tourism information, manual monitoring procedures, delayed reporting, lack of centralized promotional systems, and limited real-time analytics capabilities. The study also considers the effects of these problems on key stakeholders, including tourists, establishment owners, and tourism officers, as well as destination profiles, operational workflows, and the limitations of existing technologies currently used by the Local Government Unit and tourism-related establishments. Furthermore, constraints related to government policies, available budget, operational limitations, and project timeframe are identified to guide the scope and feasibility of the proposed system.

The \textbf{Process} phase describes the developmental procedures undertaken in the study to produce the proposed platform. This phase begins with conducting surveys, interviews, and requirement-gathering activities involving tourists, establishment owners, and tourism officers to identify operational challenges and determine user requirements. The collected data are analyzed to define both functional and non-functional system requirements. Based on the findings, the researchers proceed with the design and development of the Smart Tourism Destination Management Platform prototype, including the creation of system interfaces, centralized databases, voucher management modules, reporting dashboards, and analytics features. The developed prototype is then presented to stakeholders and evaluators for validation, testing, and feedback. The system undergoes refinement and iterative improvement based on the comments, recommendations, and usability evaluation results gathered during the testing and validation stages.

The \textbf{Output} phase represents the tangible products of the research and development process. The primary output is a functional prototype of the Smart Tourism Destination Management Platform capable of supporting tourism-related operations through centralized digital services. The system includes modules for establishment management, voucher management, tourist destination viewing, reporting, and analytics. In addition, the platform provides a centralized database for managing tourism-related records, including tourist information, establishments, voucher claims, and reports. Standardized system documentation, user manuals, operational guidelines, and technical references are also produced to support the implementation, maintenance, and future enhancement of the platform.

The \textbf{Outcome} phase reflects the expected long-term contributions and benefits of the proposed platform to tourism management in Naga City. The implementation of the system is expected to improve the efficiency, accuracy, and accessibility of tourism operations by reducing manual processes and centralizing tourism information management. The platform is also expected to strengthen coordination and communication among tourism officers, establishment owners, and tourists through a unified digital environment. Furthermore, the integration of analytics, monitoring, and AI-supported functionalities is anticipated to promote data-driven, sustainable, and innovative tourism management practices that support better decision-making, improved tourist experiences, and enhanced tourism service delivery within the city.

A feedback loop is incorporated within the framework to emphasize the iterative nature of the developmental research process. Feedback gathered from stakeholders, evaluators, and end-users during system validation and actual usage is continuously used to reassess the identified inputs, refine system processes, and improve platform outputs. This feedback mechanism ensures that the BYANAGA platform remains adaptive, user-centered, and responsive to the evolving needs of tourism management and tourism stakeholders in Naga City.

\section{Scope and Delimitation}

The objective of this research is to design and develop a web-based Smart Tourism Destination Management Platform called BYANAGA for Naga City, Camarines Sur. The platform attempts to solve three operational issues: (1) there is no unified platform where tourists can discover vouchers and promotions; (2) there is no established online digital promotion system for the local tourism businesses; and (3) disorganized coordination between the Naga City Tourism Office and the private tourism industry.

There are four distinct user roles in this platform. Tourists have an ability to create a user account, view establishment profile cards that contain the title, description, picture, and voucher badge, explore available vouchers, make use of them at registered establishments utilizing vouchers' unique codes, etc. Owners can also register business accounts, submit documents, maintain and update their profiles when approved, add and publish events, create and submit vouchers, edit/cancel vouchers awaiting for approval, check all created vouchers depending on their current statuses, get revision feedbacks, confirm tourists' voucher redemption actions in the Redemption Panel, as well as see their performance metrics like total number of vouchers used and visitors foot traffic. Tourism officer's responsibilities include approving registration of establishments, vouchers' checking and feedback providing, event management, encoding tourists' arrivals, and tourism report generation and exporting. Administrator's role involves management of all user accounts, monitoring analytics and key performance indicators dashboards for the whole city, accessing audit trail, data backup management, etc.

The research is delimited by the following parameters. First, the platform is created and analyzed as a web application alone, while mobile app development is out of the scope and considered for future research. Second, the participants are purposively chosen from the population of Naga City, and therefore generalizability to other settings is not assumed. Third, the platform is assessed using SUS and UAT on a prototype basis only, and its evaluation in practice remains beyond the present scope. Fourth, the platform lacks third-party payment gateway support, and vouchers serve as online discount certificates used offline at participating businesses. Fifth, the administrator feature is included in the platform but validated through expert review.

\section{Significance of the Study}

BYANAGA assists in the digitalization of Naga City’s tourism industry by helping fill gaps in promotion distribution, establishment-to-tourist connection, and tourism management, which have been identified as factors currently hindering the city’s competitiveness in the tourism industry.

To Tourists and the Public in General. BYANAGA offers a one-stop solution for tourists visiting Naga City who want to find promotions and vouchers offered by all of the city’s registered establishments. Tourists can now look into promotions without the hassle of going through various sources. Event listings will also assist tourists in planning their visit according to the cultural and business events in the city.

For Establishment Owners and Small Businesses. BYANAGA enables small tourism businesses to have equal opportunities in terms of a structured digital promotional channel that was not available before. Regardless of their budget constraints, registration in BYANAGA enables small and medium tourism business owners to participate in a digital promotional process governed by the tourism office where tourists can be reached, tracked, and analyzed, providing more balanced promotional opportunities and contributing to overall economic development in the region.

For the Naga City Tourism Office and Local Government Unit. BYANAGA shifts the position of the tourism office personnel from mere coordination to governance through the use of a platform. The platform makes it possible to have standardized procedures in establishing businesses, approving vouchers, managing events, and encoding visitors' data. The platform facilitates decision-making based on data analysis in developing tourism strategies consistent with the NEDA Bicol Regional Development Plan [42] and DOT digitalization mandate [54]. Moreover, the platform contributes to Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 8, 9, 11, and 17.

Recommendations for Further Researchers and System Developers. This research paper offers an adaptable template for creating online tourist destination portals within secondary cities in the Philippines and proves that digital transformation in local tourism governance can be accomplished without smart city infrastructure investment. The multi-agent design, voucher processing system led by officials, and multiple instrument assessment methods are replicable among other fragmented cities.

\section{Definition of Terms}

The following terms are defined as they are specifically applied to the design, development, and evaluation of the Smart Tourism Destination Management Platform for Naga City.

\textit{Administrator.} The user role in BYANAGA responsible for system-wide oversight, including management of all user accounts across all roles; monitoring city-wide analytics and KPI dashboards; access to the full audit trail and security logs; management of data backups and restore operations; and configuration of all system-wide settings and notification rules.

\textit{Approval Queue.} The interface within the Tourism Officer panel displaying all pending establishment registration applications and submitted vouchers awaiting review. The officer processes each item by either approving or sending revision feedback with written notes specifying required changes.

\textit{Foot Traffic Data.} Visitor presence and movement data generated through BYANAGA's redemption confirmation records and profile engagement logs, providing establishment owners and the tourism officer with insights into visitor distribution patterns across registered establishments and time periods.

\textit{Fragmented Data.} Tourism records and information scattered across multiple files, offices, or systems, resulting in inconsistencies and difficulties in generating complete and timely reports prior to the introduction of the centralized platform.

\textit{User Acceptance Testing (UAT).} The formal evaluation phase in which actual end-users, such as tourism officers and partner businesses, use the prototype to verify whether its features, usability, and performance meet practical requirements.

\textit{Centralized Database.} A single, integrated repository within the platform where all tourism-related data from different sources are stored, organized, secured, and accessed to eliminate duplication and fragmentation.

\textit{Tourist.} A registered user of the BYANAGA platform who browses establishment profiles, discovers available vouchers and promotions from all registered Naga City establishments in a single website, and redeems vouchers at registered establishments by presenting the unique voucher code at the Redemption Panel.

\textit{Tourism Officer.} The user role in BYANAGA representing the Naga City Tourism Office staff member responsible for platform governance. The tourism officer reviews establishment registration applications, approves or sends revision feedback on voucher submissions, manages city-wide events, encodes visitor arrival records, generates tourism reports, and monitors platform analytics.

\textit{Voucher.} A digital promotional offer created and submitted by an Establishment Owner through BYANAGA, reviewed and approved by the Tourism Officer, and published to tourists on the platform. Each approved voucher carries a unique redemption code. Voucher statuses are: Pending, Revision, or Approved.

\section{Research Design}
The study employed a developmental research design using a 
descriptive-developmental approach appropriate for studies involving the 
design and development of an information system prototype. The research 
focused on identifying the current issues encountered in tourism management 
in Naga City and using the gathered findings as the basis for designing the 
proposed BYANAGA Smart Tourism Management Platform.

For SOP 1, the study assessed the current state of and issues encountered 
in tourism management in Naga City. This phase involved gathering 
quantitative and qualitative data from tourists, establishment owners, and 
the tourism officer regarding existing tourism management practices, 
tourism-related operational problems, technological limitations, promotional 
management issues, reporting difficulties, and tourism information 
accessibility challenges. Survey questionnaires, interviews, and observations 
were utilized to identify the operational gaps and requirements necessary for 
the proposed platform.

For SOP 2, the study focused on the design and prototype development of the 
proposed BYANAGA Smart Tourism Management Platform. Based on the findings 
gathered during the requirement analysis phase, the researchers designed the 
proposed platform architecture, database structure, workflow processes, 
interface prototypes, and role-based modules intended for tourists, 
establishment owners, tourism officers, and administrators. System design 
artifacts such as the System Workflow Process, Use Case Diagram (UCD), Data Flow Diagram (DFD), 
Business Process Modeling Notation (BPMN), and Entity Relationship Diagram 
(ERD) were also developed to represent the structure and operational 
processes of the proposed platform.

For SOP 3, the study will evaluate the proposed BYANAGA prototype in terms 
of usability, functionality, reliability, and security. Usability will be 
assessed using the System Usability Scale (SUS), which consists of ten 
standardized statements measuring ease of use, learnability, efficiency, and 
user satisfaction, to be administered to tourist, establishment owner, and 
tourism officer respondents after a guided walkthrough of the prototype. 
Functionality will be assessed through a researcher-made evaluation 
questionnaire focused on determining whether the proposed features, modules, 
workflows, and role-based processes align with the identified tourism 
management requirements. Reliability will be assessed based on evaluator 
perception of the consistency and dependability of the proposed workflows, 
data handling processes, reporting mechanisms, and voucher management 
procedures as represented within the prototype design. Security will be 
assessed through evaluator review of the proposed user access control 
mechanisms, approval workflows, account management structure, and protection 
of tourism-related records incorporated within the prototype. All four 
dimensions will be evaluated by selected respondents and IT evaluators using 
structured questionnaires to be administered upon completion of the 
prototype.

\section{Respondents and Sampling}

The respondents of the study were composed of those who have participated in tourism operations and tourism-related services in Naga City. Purposive sampling was done when choosing the respondents since they have been selected based on their relevance to the study and their experience with tourism.

A total of thirty-six (36) respondents were identified in conducting the interviews. There were thirty (30) tourists, five (5) establishment owners, and one (1) tourism officer from the Local Government Unit of Naga City as respondents. The thirty (30) tourists were made up of people who had been, were, or had any knowledge of the tourism services within Naga City. They were considered respondents since they were essential in providing data on the common problems encountered during their searches for tourism information and plans.

Five (5) establishment owners served as respondents for the purposes of gathering data on their tourism operations and experiences in the tourism industry.

The tourism officer respondents are from the Local Government Unit Tourism Office and offered information concerning administration and operations in relation to monitoring of tourism, managing of tourism data, reporting and decision making in tourism. Inclusion of such respondents in the study made it possible for the project to acquire the required operational and end-user views.

\section{Statistical Treatment of Data}

Statistical analysis using descriptive statistics was employed to analyze the quantitative data collected through the questionnaire survey. 
Frequency and percentage distribution were used to describe the profile of the respondents. 
Weighted mean was used to determine the respondents' perceptions on the existing tourism management system, technological gaps, usefulness of the proposed BYANAGA platform, and their intention to adopt the same.
Weighted mean was also used to determine the respondents’ assessment of the proposed BYANAGA prototype in terms of usability, functionality, reliability, and security. The computed weighted mean values were interpreted using the established Likert Scale verbal interpretation table.

\subsection*{Weighted Mean}
The weighted mean was computed using the following formula:
\begin{equation*}
    \bar{x} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i x_i}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i}
\end{equation*}
where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $x_i$ = the value of each observation (scale response)
    \item $w_i$ = the weight or frequency corresponding to each observation
    \item $n$ = the total number of observations
    \item $\sum w_i x_i$ = the sum of all weighted values
    \item $\sum w_i$ = the total weight or total frequency
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Frequency and Percentage Distribution}
Frequency and percentage distribution were used to summarize the demographic and profile characteristics of both tourist and establishment respondents. Percentage was computed using the formula:
\begin{equation*}
    \% = \frac{f}{n} \times 100
\end{equation*}
where $f$ is the frequency of each category and $n$ is the total number of respondents.

\subsection*{Likert Scale and Verbal Interpretation}
A 5-point Likert Scale was used for all survey items across all respondent groups — tourists, establishment owners, and the tourism officer. The interval size was computed using the equal interval method as follows:

\end{align*}

\section{System Design and Architecture}

The BYANAGA Smart Tourism Destination Management Platform supports four different roles for users on one integrated web-based system in Naga City. These include centralized promotions discovery for the tourists, provision of establishment owners with a digital promotion mechanism under the governance of the tourism officer, and the administrator's access to full system control. It comprises three layers, namely, Presentation Layer that implements the web-based interfaces with differentiated functionality for each of the four users, Application Layer responsible for implementing the entire logic such as voucher transactions, approval queue, redemption status, analysis calculation, and reporting, and the Data Layer that ensures the storage in a persistent fashion via relational database comprising tables for establishments, vouchers, redemptions, events, arrivals, users, notifications, and logs.

    \subsection{Software Development Process}
    This study adopted an iterative software development process adapted from
    the spiral model to guide the design, development, and evaluation of the
    BYANAGA platform. The process consists of six sequential phases organized
    in a feedback loop, ensuring that each phase informs and refines the next
    until a deployable platform is produced.

    The first phase is \textbf{Gather}, where the research team collected
    functional and non-functional requirements through surveys and stakeholder
    interviews with tourists, establishment owners, and the tourism officer.
    The second phase is \textbf{Design}, where interface prototypes were
    produced for the Tourist, Establishment Owner, and Tourism Officer
    interfaces based on the gathered requirements. The third phase is
    \textbf{Build}, where all interface modules and backend logic were
    implemented based on the approved design prototypes.

    The fourth phase is \textbf{Verify}, where the platform was tested with
    the primary user groups serving as QA respondents. The fifth phase is
    \textbf{Release}, where the team deployed the BYANAGA platform to Naga
    City stakeholders for evaluation. The sixth phase is \textbf{Evaluate},
    where the team assessed the voucher management, establishment listings,
    and event capabilities of the deployed platform. A Feedback Loop connects
    the Evaluate phase back to the beginning of the cycle, ensuring that
    evaluation findings inform iterative refinements in subsequent development
    cycles.

    \subsection{Data Flow Diagram}
    A Data Flow Diagram was constructed to model how data moves through the
    BYANAGA system from external entities through internal processes to data
    stores. The diagram was developed at two levels. Level 0 presents the
    context diagram showing the four external entities and their high-level
    data exchanges with the system. Level 1 decomposes the system into five
    fundamental sub-processes: (1) User Authentication and Account Management,
    (2) Establishment Profile and Content Management, (3) Voucher Submission
    and Approval Process, (4) Voucher Redemption and Analysis Process, and
    (5) Administration and System Monitoring. Every sub-process is connected
    to an audit record data store to support system monitoring and
    accountability.

    \subsection{Use Case Diagram}

    A Use Case Diagram was developed to define the functional boundaries of
    the BYANAGA platform and document the specific interactions each external
    actor is authorized to perform within the system. The diagram was used
    during the requirements analysis phase to communicate role-based system
    functionality to both the technical development team and non-technical
    stakeholders, and served as a reference artifact during expert validation.
    The four actors identified are the Tourist, Establishment Owner, Tourism
    Officer, and Administrator, each associated with a distinct set of use
    cases that reflect their designated responsibilities within the platform.
    Sequential dependencies between related use cases are represented through
    include relationships to capture the governed flow of key platform
    processes.

    \subsection{Business Process Modeling Notation (BPMN)}

    Business Process Modeling Notation was used to model the major operational
    workflows of BYANAGA as graphical process diagrams encompassing actions,
    decisions, events, and actor interactions. BPMN was selected due to its
    standardized visual notation, which supports validation by both technical
    developers and non-technical participants including tourism officers and
    establishment owners during expert review. Four major processes were
    modeled: (1) Establishment Registration and Approval, (2) Voucher
    Submission, Review, and Publication, (3) Tourist Voucher Discovery and
    Redemption, and (4) Tourism Officer Reporting and Visitor Data Encoding.

    \subsection{Entity-Relationship Diagram}

    An Entity-Relationship Diagram was developed to model the relational
    database structure of the BYANAGA platform. The ERD was used to define
    the entities, attributes, primary keys, foreign keys, and relationships
    required to support the platform's operations, and served as the blueprint
    for database design and implementation during the Build phase. Eight main
    entities were identified: User, Establishment, Voucher, Redemption, Event,
    Visitor Record, Notification, and Audit Log, each representing a core
    component of the platform's data architecture.

\section{Validation and Testing Methods}

Expert Validation. The proposed BYANAGA prototype underwent expert validation conducted by IT experts, system developers, and academic evaluators. The evaluation focused on assessing the proposed system architecture, interface design, workflow structure, database design, and process models in terms of functionality, reliability, security, and design consistency.

Prototype Assessment. Selected respondents consisting of tourists, establishment owners, and the tourism officer evaluated the proposed prototype through structured evaluation questionnaires. The respondents assessed the proposed platform in terms of usability, functionality, reliability, and security based on the presented interface prototypes, workflow demonstrations, and proposed system features.

Usability Assessment. Usability was measured using the System Usability Scale (SUS), which evaluated the proposed platform in terms of ease of use, learnability, efficiency, and user satisfaction.

Functionality Assessment. Functionality assessment focused on determining whether the proposed features, modules, workflows, and role-based processes aligned with the identified tourism management requirements gathered during the initial phase of the study.

Reliability Assessment. Reliability assessment focused on the consistency and dependability of the proposed workflows, data handling processes, reporting mechanisms, and voucher management procedures represented within the prototype.

Security Assessment. Security assessment focused on evaluating the proposed user access control mechanisms, approval workflows, account management structure, and protection of tourism-related records incorporated within the prototype design.

Feedback Analysis. Quantitative data gathered from the SUS and prototype evaluation questionnaires were analyzed using descriptive statistical methods such as weighted mean. Qualitative feedback gathered from interviews, consultations, and open-ended responses was analyzed through thematic content analysis and used as the basis for refining the proposed prototype.

\subsubsection{Tourism Officer Interview Results}

This section presents the findings gathered through a semi-structured interview
conducted with the Tourism Officer of the Naga City Destination Management
Organization (DMO). The interview was designed to document the current state of
tourism data management, identify operational inefficiencies in reporting and
coordination workflows, and establish the specific governance requirements that
informed the design of BYANAGA's Tourism Officer interface. The findings are
organized according to the ten interview themes covered during the session.

% ============================================================
% QUESTION 1
% ============================================================

\paragraph{Current Data Collection and Storage Practices}

The Tourism Officer confirmed that tourism-related data in Naga City is
currently collected through a combination of manual and semi-digital methods.
Visitor data is primarily gathered through walk-in visitor logs recorded on the
same day of arrival, alongside reports submitted by hotels and accommodation
establishments on a periodic basis. Event registration forms --- both printed
and submitted via Google Forms --- are used during tourism events to capture
attendance figures. Collected data is then transferred and stored in spreadsheets
for consolidation purposes. Printed documents and logbooks serve as the primary
physical backup for records that have not yet been digitized. This multi-source,
multi-format data collection approach results in fragmented records that are
difficult to consolidate efficiently, particularly during high-volume tourism
periods when the volume of incoming data from various sources peaks
simultaneously.

% ============================================================
% QUESTION 2
% ============================================================

\paragraph{Types of Records Managed}

The Tourism Officer manages a broad range of tourism-related records as part
of the DMO's regular operations. These include tourist arrival data, destination
records covering both the database of registered tourist attractions and
operational details of individual sites, event records for tourism activities
within the city, accommodation and hotel reports submitted by registered
establishments, establishment records for businesses operating within the
tourism sector, tourist feedback collected during and after visits, permits
and accreditation documents for tourism-related businesses, and monthly tourism
reports that are prepared and submitted to the Department of Tourism (DOT). The
breadth of record types managed by a single officer underscores the operational
complexity of the DMO's data management responsibilities and highlights the
need for a centralized system capable of organizing multiple record categories
within a single accessible interface --- a need that directly informed the
design of BYANAGA's Tourism Officer panel, which consolidates establishment
management, voucher governance, visitor data encoding, event management, and
report generation into one integrated workflow environment.

% ============================================================
% QUESTION 3
% ============================================================

\paragraph{Current Tools and Systems in Use}

When asked about the tools and systems currently used for data management, the
Tourism Officer indicated that the DMO relies primarily on a combination of
paper forms and printed logbooks for recording visitor arrivals and tourism
activities, supplemented by Google Forms for specific event-related data
collection. Spreadsheets serve as the main consolidation tool for aggregating
data from multiple sources. The local government unit operates an existing
system called eTRACS, though its application to tourism data management is
limited. Reports are also received from establishments in various formats,
further adding to the consolidation burden. The absence of a unified,
purpose-built tourism data management system means that the officer must manually
transfer data between these disparate tools before any reporting or analysis
can take place. This workflow is both time-intensive and prone to transcription
errors, particularly when consolidating data from physical logbooks into digital
spreadsheets.

% ============================================================
% QUESTION 4
% ============================================================

\paragraph{Problems Encountered in Preparing Tourism Reports}

The Tourism Officer identified several recurring problems in the report
preparation process. Incomplete data was cited as a primary concern, as not
all establishments and accommodation providers submit their reports on time or
in consistent formats, resulting in gaps in the consolidated data set. Late
submission of reports from partner establishments compounds this problem by
compressing the available time for data consolidation before DMO reporting
deadlines. Manual counting of visitor records --- particularly for walk-in logs
and event attendance sheets --- is a time-consuming process that limits the
officer's capacity to attend to other operational responsibilities. The overall
consolidation process, which requires aggregating data from multiple physical
and digital sources, was described as significantly time-consuming. The lack of
real-time updates across data sources means that the officer has no immediate
visibility into current tourism activity levels, making it difficult to respond
proactively to emerging trends or operational issues. As the officer noted, the
common problems encountered are incomplete data, late report submission from
partner establishments, and the time-consuming nature of manual data
consolidation without automated support.

% ============================================================
% QUESTION 5
% ============================================================

\paragraph{Time Required for Report Preparation}

The Tourism Officer confirmed that preparing a complete tourism report
typically requires one week or more from the point of data collection to
final submission. This extended preparation period is attributable to the
manual consolidation of data from multiple sources, the need to follow up with
establishments for missing or incomplete submissions, and the absence of
automated report generation tools. A report preparation cycle of one week or
more represents a significant operational bottleneck in the DMO's workflows,
as it delays the availability of tourism performance data for decision-making
and creates a recurring workload burden that limits the officer's capacity for
other governance responsibilities. This finding directly validates BYANAGA's
automated report generation feature, which enables the Tourism Officer to
generate, review, and export reports in real time from consolidated database
records, eliminating the manual data gathering and consolidation cycle
entirely.

% ============================================================
% QUESTION 6
% ============================================================

\paragraph{Information Needed for Tourism-Related Decisions}

When asked what information is needed to support tourism-related decisions,
the Tourism Officer identified a comprehensive set of data requirements. These
include the total number of visitors by period, the most visited and least
visited destinations within the city, event attendance figures, peak months
and tourism seasons, tourist feedback and satisfaction data, establishment
activity levels and compliance with reporting requirements, accommodation
availability and occupancy rates, complaints and tourist concerns, and
weather or safety-related information that may affect visitor activity. The
breadth of this information requirement confirms that effective tourism
governance depends on access to multiple intersecting data streams that are
currently collected, stored, and managed separately. BYANAGA's centralized
analytics dashboard --- which aggregates visitor trends, voucher redemption
data, establishment performance metrics, and event activity within a single
interface --- was designed to address precisely this multi-dimensional
information need, providing the Tourism Officer with a consolidated view of
the data required for informed operational and policy decisions.

% ============================================================
% QUESTION 7
% ============================================================

\paragraph{Difficulties in Monitoring Tourist Activities and Establishments}

The Tourism Officer identified several persistent difficulties in monitoring
tourist activities and the compliance of registered establishments. The
absence of a real-time visitor count mechanism means that the officer has no
immediate visibility into how many tourists are present in the city at any
given time, relying instead on periodic reports that reflect past activity
rather than current conditions. Establishments do not submit updates
regularly, creating chronic gaps in the DMO's records of establishment
operational status, pricing, and promotional activity. Tracking active events
across the city is similarly challenging, as there is no unified calendar or
notification system through which establishment owners and the DMO
communicate event information. Limited staff capacity within the tourism
office compounds these challenges, as the officer must manage monitoring
responsibilities alongside data encoding, report preparation, and stakeholder
coordination with a small team. Delayed reports from partner establishments
and accommodation providers further reduce the currency and completeness of
the data available for monitoring purposes. These findings directly informed
BYANAGA's design of the Tourism Officer panel, which addresses each of these
monitoring challenges through the establishment validation queue, the voucher
review workflow, the event management module, and the visitor data encoding
interface --- all integrated within a single governance panel that reduces
coordination overhead and improves the timeliness and completeness of
tourism data.

% ============================================================
% QUESTION 8
% ============================================================

\paragraph{Dashboard Features That Would Improve Operations}

The Tourism Officer expressed strong interest in a range of dashboard features
that would support more efficient and informed DMO operations. The most
frequently mentioned features included a real-time total visitor count, a
listing and ranking of top destinations by visitor volume, event monitoring
tools providing visibility into upcoming and ongoing tourism activities,
a comprehensive establishment list with operational status indicators,
monthly visitor trend charts and graph summaries, date and destination
filtering capabilities for targeted data analysis, automatic report
generation eliminating the need for manual consolidation, downloadable
reports in standard formats for DOT submission, alerts and notifications
for pending tasks and new submissions, and a map-based destination viewer
providing a spatial overview of tourist distribution across the city. These
feature preferences are directly reflected in BYANAGA's Tourism Officer
panel design, where the analytics dashboard, report generation module,
event management tools, and notification system collectively address
each of the capabilities identified by the officer as operationally
necessary. The alignment between the officer's expressed needs and the
platform's implemented features confirms that BYANAGA's design is grounded
in the documented operational realities of the Naga City DMO rather than
assumed requirements.

% ============================================================
% QUESTION 9
% ============================================================

\paragraph{Decisions That Would Benefit from AI-Generated Insights}

When asked which governance decisions would benefit from AI-generated
insights or recommendations, the Tourism Officer identified several
high-value applications. Predicting visitor trends based on historical
arrival and activity data would enable proactive resource planning for
anticipated peak periods. Identifying high-activity destinations would
support targeted infrastructure investment and promotional prioritization.
AI-powered recommendations for destinations to feature in promotional
campaigns would improve the strategic targeting of the DMO's marketing
efforts. Detecting low tourist activity at specific destinations or
during particular periods would allow the officer to initiate responsive
promotional interventions before inactivity becomes entrenched. Suggesting
optimal event planning periods based on historical visitor patterns would
improve event attendance and reduce scheduling conflicts with peak tourism
windows. Identifying possible overcrowding risks at popular destinations
would enable the officer to implement crowd management measures in advance
rather than reactively. These AI feature preferences confirm the Tourism
Officer's openness to data-driven governance tools and validate the
AI analytics direction identified as a future development priority in
BYANAGA's scope and delimitation. The current platform implementation
provides the foundational data collection and aggregation infrastructure
from which AI-powered features can be developed in subsequent iterations.

% QUESTION 10

\paragraph{Reports the System Should Automatically Generate}

The Tourism Officer specified a comprehensive list of reports that an
automated tourism management system should be capable of generating.
These include monthly visitor reports aggregating total arrivals, origins,
and visit patterns for the reporting period; annual tourism summary reports
providing a full-year overview of tourism performance for policy planning
and DOT submission; destination performance reports documenting visitor
volumes and activity levels at individual tourist sites and registered
establishments; event attendance reports capturing participation figures
for tourism events within the city; establishment reports summarizing the
operational status, voucher activity, and compliance levels of registered
businesses; tourist feedback reports aggregating satisfaction ratings and
review content from platform users; peak season reports identifying the
months and periods of highest tourist activity; low-activity destination
reports flagging sites with consistently below-average visitor volumes;
and tourism KPI reports measuring performance against the targets set
by the DMO and the city government for tourism development objectives.
The specificity and breadth of the Tourism Officer's reporting requirements
confirm that BYANAGA's report generation module --- which supports
multiple report types with date range and category filters and exports
in PDF and CSV formats --- must be designed to accommodate a diverse
range of reporting needs within a single flexible interface. These
requirements also establish the data model requirements that informed
the BYANAGA database architecture, ensuring that the Visitor Record,
Establishment, Voucher, Redemption, Event, and Review entities store
the attributes necessary to populate each of the report types identified
by the officer.

\paragraph{Summary of Tourism Officer Interview Findings}

Semi-Structured Interview with Tourism Officer of the Naga City DMO
yielded seven main findings that provided valuable information on the design
of BYANAGA application. First, tourism information in Naga City is gathered
through fragmented sources, such as walk-in logs, hotel information, Google
Forms, spreadsheets, and paper log books without any mechanism for aggregation.
Second, the tourism officer handles various types of records related to tourists,
travel destinations, tourism events, establishments, permits, and monthly DOT

Figure~\ref{fig:dfd0} presents the Data Flow Diagram Level 0 of BYANAGA,
showing the system as a single central process and illustrating how data flows

\textbf{Tourist.}
The Tourist sends three inputs into the system: Registration Data when
creating a new account, Voucher Claim Request when selecting a voucher from
the marketplace, and Rating and Review after redeeming a voucher at an
establishment. In return, the system sends back three outputs to the Tourist:

Confirmed as acknowledgment that a voucher was successfully claimed, and
Destination Info containing tourism destination details and travel tips
available on the platform.

\textbf{Establishment Owner.}
The Establishment Owner sends three inputs into the system: Business Details
containing the establishment profile and contact information, Supporting
Documents required for registration validation, and Voucher Details containing
the promotional voucher information submitted for officer review. The system
returns three outputs to the Establishment Owner: Approval Status notifying
the owner of the result of their registration review, Voucher Feedback
containing revision notes from the Tourism Officer when a submitted voucher
requires corrections, and Performance Summary providing the owner with
redemption counts and foot traffic analytics from their dashboard.

\textbf{Tourism Officer.}
The Tourism Officer sends three inputs into the system: Voucher Review
Decision indicating whether a submitted voucher is approved or requires
revision, Destination Data containing tourist destination information being
added or updated on the platform, and Report Request when generating a tourism
report. The system returns two outputs to the Tourism Officer: Voucher
Submission representing the pending voucher submissions from establishment
owners that the officer must review, and Generated Report which is the
completed tourism report produced from the system's data in the requested
format.

\textbf{Administrator.}
The Administrator sends three inputs into the system: Approve or Reject
Establishment representing the validation decision on pending establishment
registration submissions, Officer Account Data when creating a new Tourism
Officer account, and Report Request when requesting system-wide analytics
reports. The system returns four outputs to the Administrator: Pending
Registrations showing the list of establishment registration applications
awaiting review, Voucher Analytics providing system-wide data on voucher
performance across all establishments, and Generated Report containing the
compiled analytics report requested by the Administrator.

Overall, the diagram demonstrates that the BYANAGA system serves as the
central processing hub connecting all four external entities through structured
and role-specific data exchanges. Each entity interacts with the system only
through data flows that correspond to their assigned role, ensuring that
tourists access promotional and destination content, establishment owners
manage their profiles and vouchers, the Tourism Officer governs content
approval and reporting, and the Administrator oversees user management and
system-wide analytics. The Level 0 diagram confirms that all interactions pass
through the centralized system, establishing BYANAGA as a single integrated
platform for Naga City tourism management.

\subsubsection{Entity-Relationship Diagram}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/ERD.png}
    \caption{Entity-Relationship Diagram of BYANAGA (Crow's Foot Notation)}
    \label{fig:erd}
\end{figure}

The Entity Relationship Diagram of BYANAGA describes the relational database through eleven core entities: Administrator, Tourism Officer, Establishment Owner, Establishment, Tourist Destination, Voucher, Tourist, Voucher Claim, Review, Notification, and Audit Trail.
The role of Administrators is to set up officer accounts and validate establishment entries. Tourism Officers are responsible for management of tourist destinations and review of vouchers. An Establishment Owner is associated with one or more Establishments, holding their names, categories, locations, permit number, validation status, and uploaded documents. Vouchers are tied to Establishments with discount information, expiration dates, number of claims possible, statuses, and officer remarks. Tourists claim their vouchers via the Voucher Claim, indicating the voucher's redemption and the timestamp. Reviews keep record of rating, message, and keywords left by tourists post-redeeming vouchers. Locations of Tourist Destinations contain the geographical information, coordinates, working hours, and travel tips managed by Officers. Notifications capture system messages sent out for particular users and roles. Audit Trail records all activities performed within the system along with its actor, action, target, and timestamp.
The ERD thus establishes the relational framework on which the proposed system functions.
